How to start a daily quiet time.

A quiet time is a short, unhurried sitting with Scripture, on your own, most days. It is not a study and not a performance. This is the shape of one, in six steps, in about fifteen minutes. You can follow it with a paper Bible and a pen and never install anything.

Written by the person who builds Cardinal, which is shaped around this method.

Why most attempts end in two weeks

People rarely give up because they stop caring. They give up for three reasons, and all three are fixable before you start.

The passage is too big. "Read a chapter a day" sounds modest until the chapter is Numbers 7, which is a hundred and eighty-nine verses of identical offerings. Chapters were added to the Bible in the thirteenth century. They are a navigation aid, not a serving size.

There is no shape. Opening the book and waiting to feel something is a lot to ask of a tired person at 6am. Having the same six steps every day removes the part where you decide how to begin.

A missed day ends it. Anything that counts consecutive days turns one bad week into a reason to quit. If you are tracking a streak, you are being managed by a number rather than reading.

The six steps

1. Pause

Sit still for about thirty seconds before you read a word. Put the phone face down or out of reach. This feels like the skippable step and it is the one that makes the rest work: you are arriving, not starting a task. If it helps, say one sentence, out loud or not, along the lines of I am here, and I am listening.

2. Read

Read a scene, not a chapter. Fifteen to twenty-five verses is about right: one parable, one confrontation, one argument in a letter, one psalm. Find where the action or the thought actually starts and stops, and read that.

Read it slowly, and read it twice. The second pass is where you notice things. If you can, read one of them aloud; it slows you to roughly speaking speed, which is close to how the text was first heard.

3. Reflect

Ask three questions in this order, and do not skip to the third.

  1. What is actually happening here? Who is speaking, to whom, and what has just happened before this.
  2. What does this say about God? Not about you yet. What is He like in this passage.
  3. What surprises me, bothers me, or does not fit? The friction is usually the door. A verse you find uncomfortable is a better use of ten minutes than one you already agree with.

Write one or two sentences. Not a summary, just the thing you noticed. Writing is what turns reading into remembering.

4. Apply

One thing, today, specific enough to fail at. "Be more patient" is not an application. "Do not interrupt my daughter when she tells me about her day" is.

Most days there will not be a dramatic one, and inventing a task so the step feels complete is worse than leaving it. On those days the honest application is often simply to remember what you read.

5. Pray

Pray the passage back. If it showed you something about God, say that back to Him. If it showed you something about yourself, say that. If you have no words, reading the psalm you just read as a prayer is a very old and entirely legitimate way through.

Then add whatever is actually on your mind. Keep a running list of what you are praying for, on paper or in an app, and mark things answered when they are. Six months of that list is a quietly overwhelming document.

6. Close

Stop deliberately. Close the book, take a breath, and go. Ending on purpose is what keeps the sitting from dribbling into checking your email in the same posture, which teaches your body that this was never really a separate thing.

If you have ten minutes, not fifteen

Keep pause, read and pray, and let reflect and apply collapse into one question: what is this saying about God, and what do I want to say back? A short real sitting beats a long one you skip.

Practical questions people actually ask

When should I do it?

Whenever you will genuinely do it. Morning is traditional and suits people whose evenings are unpredictable. If you are not a morning person, an evening sitting is not a lesser version; it is the one that will still be happening in March.

Which translation?

For a daily sitting, a plain modern English translation you will not have to decode. The King James is magnificent and is not the easiest place to start if English is your first language and 1611 is not. Read a more literal translation alongside it when a verse gets strange.

Where should I start reading?

Not Genesis 1 with the intention of reading straight through. That plan dies in Leviticus, reliably. Better first stops:

  • The Gospel of John, if you want to meet Jesus directly. Twenty-one chapters, mostly scenes.
  • The Psalms, if you are in a hard season. They say the difficult things out loud so you do not have to find the words.
  • Philippians, if you want something short, warm and complete in two weeks.
  • Mark, if you want the fastest account of what Jesus did.

What if I miss a week?

You pick up at the next reading. That is the whole answer. Nothing has been lost and there is nothing to make up. A practice that punishes gaps is not a practice, it is a subscription you feel bad about.
